# Fallibility, Synthetic Approximation, and Ethical Redress

**Classification:** Personal Research Ethics  
**Subject:** Generative text, coincidental resemblance, excluded material, ethical recognition, contaminant ejection, and mediation  
**Status:** Companion statement to the Self-Doctrine  
**Source basis:** The individual’s request for this page and explicit approval of the proposed interpretation and repository scope.

[← Return to the reading hub](README.md) · [Read the full Self-Doctrine](SELF_DOCTRINE_EXTENDED.md)

> **Resemblance requires examination. Recognition requires a response. Permission remains bounded.**

## I. Purpose and Interpretive Status

This page develops the individual’s stated position on fallibility: an output may resemble restricted work without deliberate derivation, and a person may recognize an ethical boundary only after their attention has carried them across it.

These possibilities require careful assessment. Neither resemblance nor a claim of coincidence settles the history of a work. Neither an account of delayed recognition nor a declaration of good intent settles the remedy owed.

The source statement is a personal ethical declaration. The assistant’s contribution is editorial: organizing the approved distinctions and explaining their implications. This page does not provide a diagnosis, establish a medical explanation, determine a legal dispute, or attest to the origin of any particular output.

It supplements the doctrine’s treatment of [evidence](SELF_DOCTRINE_EXTENDED.md#viii-epistemic-classes-and-claim-discipline), [uncertain conceptual debt](SELF_DOCTRINE_EXTENDED.md#xii-derivation-incubation-and-uncertain-conceptual-debt), and [correction](SELF_DOCTRINE_EXTENDED.md#xvi-revocation-correction-and-derived-material).

## II. Synthetic Text and Approximation

Generative text should not be treated as a transparent record of the process that produced it. A fluent passage may appear to express an intended idea accurately while leaving the pathway of influence unresolved.

An approximation may resemble a phrase, construction, argument, or implementation without demonstrating that its producer recovered the originating person’s interior method. Resemblance to an intended result does not independently establish access to that intent.

Likewise, a system’s explanation of its own output should not be accepted as a complete provenance record merely because the explanation is coherent. A statement of independence needs support appropriate to that claim.

Instructions excluding a source establish a boundary for the activity. They do not, by themselves, demonstrate that every resulting construction is independent of that source. Exclusion from a particular input also does not establish what material may have influenced a system elsewhere.

These distinctions concern the limits of what can be concluded. They do not establish that any particular system encountered a particular source.

## III. Constrained Construction and Coincidental Convergence

Expression is shaped by vocabulary, grammar, conventions, task requirements, available tools, and the need to communicate an intelligible result. Different people and systems may therefore arrive at similar constructions through shared constraints or coincidence.

Language permits indefinitely many combinations. The relevant constraint is narrower: for a given purpose, length, medium, and set of requirements, the available plausible or conventional constructions may overlap substantially.

A short functional phrase, a standard algorithmic step, or a conventional arrangement may offer fewer meaningful alternatives than its surrounding language suggests. Similarity in such a case requires a different evidential assessment from a long, unusual correspondence.

No numerical probability of coincidence is asserted here. The existence of shared constraints does not establish that a particular resemblance occurred by chance, and it does not make every resemblance equally likely.

The governing distinctions are:

- Similarity alone does not prove copying, access, appropriation, or intent.
- A claim of coincidence alone does not prove independent derivation.
- Excluding a source does not guarantee that no similar result can arise.
- A similar result does not establish recovery of a person’s methodology.
- Where the available evidence cannot distinguish competing explanations, the derivational status may remain unresolved.

## IV. Excluded and Forbidden Implementation Material

Material designated as “anti-implementation” or “forbidden implementation material” remains outside the authorized activity according to the restriction actually given.

Those expressions do not acquire a universal technical meaning through this page. Their operative meaning must come from the governing source, the person setting the boundary, and the agreed scope.

A restriction must not be converted into a route for deliberately reconstructing the prohibited result. Rephrasing, indirect prompting, or changing the medium does not supply missing permission.

At the same time, an independently occurring resemblance should not automatically be classified as circumvention. The assessment should distinguish the instruction, actual source access, known dependence, resulting similarities, and the limits of the available record.

If possible unauthorized dependence becomes apparent, the appropriate response is to pause the affected use and assess it. Automatic condemnation and unsupported declarations of independence both exceed what resemblance alone establishes.

## V. Personal Declaration: Ethics, Mental Illness, and Recognition

The individual’s position, expressed here in edited form, is:

> Mental illness does not cause me to abandon my ethics. I may nevertheless become fascinated by something before I realize that I have crossed an ethical boundary. When I recognize that crossing, my commitment is to address it and eject the contaminant.

This is a statement about the individual’s ethical commitments and their account of delayed recognition. It is not an independently verified finding about causation, cognition, or conduct.

The declaration distinguishes adherence to an ethical standard from infallible awareness of every boundary. A person may maintain a standard while acknowledging that they can fail to recognize a particular crossing promptly.

The account should not be inflated into a claim of perfect conduct. It should also not be transformed into an assumption that mental illness invalidates the individual’s ethics, consent, or present statement.

Within this doctrine, delayed recognition calls for correction when the issue becomes apparent. Fascination does not itself authorize continued reliance on material once its use is recognized as outside the permitted scope.

## VI. Redress Through Ejection of Contaminants

“Contaminant” refers here to material or influence identified as unauthorized within the work under examination. It does not refer to a person, a diagnosis, or an undesirable personal characteristic.

“Ejection” means ceasing reliance on that material and addressing the affected work. The appropriate response depends upon what is known, what can be separated, and what authority exists to make changes.

A proportionate response may include:

1. Stopping the affected use or distribution.
2. Isolating the affected portion while its status is assessed.
3. Identifying known dependencies and preserving uncertainty about others.
4. Removing or replacing unauthorized contributions, or rebuilding affected work where necessary and authorized.
5. Correcting attribution or descriptions of independence where the record supports correction.
6. Recording the remedy and its limits without unnecessarily duplicating protected material.

Suspicion should remain identifiable as suspicion. Material should not be declared unauthorized solely because it resembles another work.

A declaration of ejection does not prove that every influence has disappeared. Where an influence cannot be reliably separated or its removal cannot be demonstrated, the limitation should remain explicit. An output may need to remain withdrawn or unresolved rather than being presented as clean through assertion.

Correction should not silently erase the history necessary to understand the issue. Any retention of that history must itself respect confidentiality, consent, and applicable deletion conditions.

This statement documents a redress principle. It does not authorize deletion of existing records, destruction of evidence, or changes to other people’s material.

## VII. Voluntary Transfer and Mediation

The individual’s ethical position is that a transfer of intellectual property occurring without force remains open to mediation.

“Always mediatable” expresses a commitment to keeping a route to voluntary clarification and resolution available. It does not guarantee that the parties will participate, agree, or reach a legally effective settlement.

Absence of force does not independently establish informed consent, ownership, authority to transfer, or agreement about scope. Those matters may themselves be the subjects of mediation.

Depending on the disagreement, mediation may address:

- What was contributed, disclosed, licensed, assigned, or understood to be transferred.
- Whether permission existed and what it covered.
- Attribution and the representation of conceptual debt.
- Future use, restricted use, withdrawal, or correction.
- Retention, recipients, and derivative material.
- A mutually accepted remedy for an identified boundary crossing.

A transfer’s terms must not be silently expanded by later usefulness. A settlement must not be treated as the person’s endorsement of every account of the original event.

Mediation can support prospective agreement and redress. It does not retroactively turn missing consent into consent that existed at the time. Any later authorization should be recorded as later authorization.

This is an ethical commitment to a process of resolution, not a claim that every dispute is legally mediable or that any party can be compelled to accept proposed terms.

## VIII. Consolidated Principles

| Distinction | Consequence |
| :--- | :--- |
| Approximation is not provenance. | A plausible output does not disclose its full derivational history. |
| Shared constraints permit convergence. | Similar constructions can arise without deliberate copying; a particular case still requires evidence. |
| Exclusion is not proof of independence. | A restriction and its successful observance are separate matters. |
| Coincidence is a possibility, not an automatic verdict. | Neither accusation nor exoneration follows from resemblance alone. |
| Ethical commitment is compatible with fallibility. | Delayed recognition calls for an accountable response. |
| Ejection requires a bounded remedy. | Cease reliance, correct affected work, and state what remains uncertain. |
| Voluntary transfer remains open to mediation. | Clarification and remedy are possible without presuming agreement. |
| Later agreement is not earlier consent. | The chronology of permission must remain visible. |

## IX. Final Statement

The possibility of coincidental approximation protects inquiry from unsupported accusations. The duty to examine known dependence protects that possibility from becoming an excuse.

A person’s ethical commitment does not require a claim of infallibility. It requires attention to boundaries, willingness to recognize error, and a response proportionate to what has been established.

Where unauthorized material is identified, reliance should cease and the affected work should be corrected within the authority available. Where the history cannot be responsibly established, uncertainty should remain visible.

Where a transfer is non-coercive, the individual’s commitment is to keep clarification, negotiation, and remedy open. Consent, attribution, correction, and settlement retain their distinct meanings throughout that process.

**Fallibility requires room for correction. Correction requires honesty about both the remedy and its limits.**
