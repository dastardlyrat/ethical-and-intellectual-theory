# Self-Doctrine: Intellectual Methodology, Derivation, and Personal Attribution

**Classification:** Personal Research Ethics  
**Subject:** Intellectual work, computational analysis, methodological fingerprints, consent, attribution, and archival restrictions  
**Status:** Doctrinal statement — methodology intentionally undisclosed  
**Governing condition:** The extraction and archival of an identifying methodological profile require the individual’s explicit, specifically scoped consent.

## I. Foundational Principle

The publication of source code, algorithms, or other intellectual work does not constitute unrestricted ethical consent to extract, reconstruct, profile, or archive the creator’s characteristic methods of operation.

A distinction must be maintained between examining a work and constructing an account of the person through that work. An implementation may reveal how a computational problem was solved. A systematic analysis of recurring choices may seek something further: a reproducible characterization of how a particular individual thinks, organizes, derives, or resolves problems.

These are distinguishable objects of inquiry. Permission concerning one does not automatically establish permission concerning the other.

The ethical boundary therefore depends upon more than the accessibility of the source. It also concerns the purpose of examination, the information derived, its association with an individual, and the uses for which it is preserved.

Public availability establishes an opportunity for observation. It does not, by itself, establish consent to every form of inference that observation makes possible.

This doctrine recognizes legitimate study of intellectual work while asserting a separate requirement for the deliberate reconstruction and retention of an identifying intellectual profile. Such activity may be permitted when the individual gives explicit consent covering the proposed activity and its intended uses.

## II. Progressive Boundary Violation

An ethical violation may arise from several distinct actions. Its seriousness increases when those actions accumulate, reinforce one another, or extend beyond the circumstances in which access was originally granted.

### 1. Boundary transgression

Boundary transgression occurs when expressly communicated restrictions concerning examination, profiling, derivation, or retention are disregarded.

A restriction concerning one activity should be understood according to its stated scope. Permission to inspect an implementation, for example, does not automatically include permission to create a persistent profile of its author.

Likewise, consent to a particular analysis does not automatically extend to unrelated analyses, additional recipients, or future applications.

The relevant question is whether the activity undertaken falls within the permission actually given.

### 2. Methodological fingerprint extraction

For the purposes of this doctrine, a methodological fingerprint is a reconstructed characterization of an individual’s distinctive intellectual practices that is associated with, or intended to be attributable to, that individual.

The concern extends beyond noticing that a work uses familiar algorithms, common conventions, or established techniques. It arises when analysis deliberately assembles characteristics into an account of the individual’s recurring intellectual behavior.

No single observed choice necessarily establishes a distinctive methodology. A shared convention does not become a personal identifier merely because an individual uses it. Claims that a profile is distinctive or attributable must therefore remain distinguishable from the observations on which those claims depend.

The ethical concern does not require the resulting profile to be perfectly accurate. An uncertain or mistaken characterization may still intrude upon the individual’s informational boundaries when it is retained and used as an account of that person.

### 3. Conceptual appropriation

Conceptual appropriation concerns the relationship between an originating contribution and subsequent work substantially dependent upon it.

A later work may contain substantial effort while still owing a material conceptual debt to an earlier contributor. Changes in terminology, presentation, programming language, or surrounding structure do not, by themselves, resolve that debt.

Transparent attribution should make the originating contribution understandable. Claims of independent derivation should likewise distinguish what was independently established from what was learned, adapted, or inferred from another person’s work.

Attribution and consent address separate obligations. Acknowledging the source does not automatically authorize the extraction of a personal methodological profile. Consent to examination does not automatically excuse the concealment of conceptual dependence.

### 4. Persistent archival

Persistent archival occurs when an extracted profile is retained, indexed, correlated, transferred, or otherwise preserved for subsequent use.

Retention introduces a continuing ethical consequence. A profile developed for one occasion may become available for later identification, comparison, imitation, or exploitation. Those later uses may exceed the purpose originally described to the individual.

Archival therefore requires its own consideration within the scope of consent. Permission to conduct an analysis should not be silently treated as permission to preserve every resulting characterization indefinitely.

These four conditions can operate independently. Their combination creates a progressively more serious breach because the activity may disregard an expressed boundary, extract personal characteristics, obscure intellectual dependence, and preserve the result for further use.

## III. Personal Intellectual Property

For the purposes of this doctrine, an individual’s distinctive intellectual methodology is recognized as an interest deserving ethical protection independently of the ordinary licensing status of the works through which it becomes observable.

The expression **personal intellectual property** names that asserted ethical interest. It does not presume that every characteristic, habit, inference, or method is independently protected under existing intellectual-property law.

The interest concerns a person’s relationship to the reconstruction and use of their intellectual identity. It includes whether a profile is created, how it is represented, what purposes it serves, and whether it is preserved or transferred.

This position does not assign personal ownership over all ideas an individual encounters or employs. Established mathematics, shared conventions, functional requirements, and independently derived solutions require their own assessment. Their occurrence in a person’s work is insufficient, by itself, to establish exclusive personal control over them.

The protected concern is the deliberate construction and use of an attributable profile of the individual.

Within this doctrine, systematic archival of such a profile without the individual’s explicit consent constitutes an intrusion into personal intellectual property. Where consent is given, the permitted activity remains bounded by what the individual authorized.

## IV. The Genetic Information Analogy

The ethical concern is analogous to extracting and archiving an individual’s genetic information.

The availability of material from which identifying information can be derived does not, by itself, resolve whether that derivation, retention, or subsequent use is ethically authorized.

Source code presents a comparable distinction between an accessible object and further information inferred about its creator. An implementation can be examined for its computational behavior. It can also become material for an attempt to characterize the individual behind it.

The analogy concerns the transition from access to inference, and from inference to a durable personal record. It draws attention to autonomy, consent, informational boundaries, and the possibility that retained information may support uses beyond the original encounter.

The analogy has limits. Source code and genetic information are not asserted to be scientifically equivalent, equally identifying, or governed by identical legal protections. Intellectual practices can also reflect collaboration, instruction, common tools, inherited conventions, and changing circumstances.

Those limitations constrain what may responsibly be claimed about an inferred profile. They do not eliminate the separate ethical question of whether constructing and preserving that profile was authorized.

## V. Permissible Research Boundary

This doctrine permits legitimate investigation of computational problems, publicly established mathematical principles, functional requirements, algorithms, and alternative implementations.

It also recognizes that studying a work may necessarily involve identifying patterns, comparing approaches, and explaining design decisions. Pattern recognition alone does not establish that an identifying personal profile has been extracted.

The research boundary must be evaluated through the relationship among purpose, information, attribution, permission, and use.

Relevant questions include:

- Is the inquiry directed toward understanding the computational problem, the implementation, or the individual?
- Does the resulting account describe the work’s behavior, or does it claim to characterize the creator’s recurring intellectual identity?
- What conceptual contributions came from the source, and how are those contributions acknowledged?
- What did the individual authorize?
- Will personal characterizations be retained, combined with other information, transferred, or reused?

For example, explaining why an implementation fails under a particular input may remain an analysis of the implementation. Constructing a retained account intended to recognize or reproduce its author’s characteristic reasoning introduces a separate personal-profiling purpose.

Likewise, independently solving the same functional problem does not establish appropriation merely because some choices coincide. Where substantial dependence exists, however, it should be represented accurately.

These distinctions require attention to the actual activity. Describing a project as research does not settle its ethical status. Neither does the mere presence of similarity establish a violation.

Legitimate inquiry remains possible when its claims are proportionate to its evidence, its conceptual dependencies are transparent, and any personal profiling remains within explicit consent.

## VI. Archival Prohibition and Consent

Without the individual’s explicit, specifically scoped consent, this doctrine prohibits the archival of their identifying methodological fingerprint.

Research necessity, analytical convenience, a permissive source-code license, or technological capability does not independently supply that consent.

**Where the individual gives consent, the activity may proceed within the scope authorized.** Consent should make the proposed activity sufficiently understandable for the individual to decide what they are permitting.

For the purposes of applying this doctrine, the scope should distinguish:

- The material to be examined.
- The purpose and nature of the personal characterization.
- Whether the characterization may be retained, and for what period.
- Who may access or receive it.
- The uses for which it may be employed.
- Whether further correlation, transfer, or reuse is included.

These distinctions prevent permission for one activity from becoming an unlimited authorization through implication.

Consent to a bounded evaluation does not automatically authorize indefinite retention. Consent to retention does not automatically authorize publication, transfer, or unrelated use. An activity outside the agreed scope requires additional consent.

Any conditions governing later withdrawal, deletion, or the handling of already-derived material should be stated when consent is established. This doctrine does not presume that those consequences have been resolved merely because initial permission exists.

Consent also does not establish that a profile is accurate, scientifically valid, or fairly represented. Authorization and evidential validity remain separate questions.

The prohibited object, absent consent, is the reconstructed, attributable intellectual profile of an individual. Ordinary computational knowledge does not become prohibited solely because it was learned through study.

This doctrine intentionally discloses no procedures for producing methodological fingerprints.

## VII. Final Axiom

**The freedom to study a work is not equivalent to the freedom to appropriate, reproduce, or archive the intellectual identity of its creator.**

The ethical assessment of research must consider what it extracts, whose characteristics it claims to describe, how its conceptual dependencies are represented, and what it preserves for future use.

Expressed boundaries require attention. Attribution requires honesty. Personal profiling and archival require explicit consent appropriate to their scope.

Where consent is given, permission extends to the authorized activity. Where consent is absent, access to the source does not supply the missing authorization.

The more extensively an activity disregards expressed boundaries, depends upon extracted personal characteristics, obscures its conceptual debt, or preserves an identifying profile beyond the permission given, the greater the ethical violation.

A research process must therefore be evaluated through both its resulting work and its treatment of the person whose intellectual activity made that work possible.
