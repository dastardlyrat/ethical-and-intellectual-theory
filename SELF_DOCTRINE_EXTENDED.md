# Self-Doctrine: Intellectual Methodology, Derivation, and Personal Attribution

**Classification:** Personal Research Ethics  
**Subject:** Intellectual work, computational analysis, methodological fingerprints, consent, attribution, derivation, artificial-intelligence adaptation, and archival restrictions  
**Status:** Expanded doctrinal statement — methodology intentionally undisclosed  
**Governing condition:** The extraction, reconstruction, operationalization, or archival of an identifying methodological profile requires the individual’s explicit, specifically scoped consent.

## Provenance and Interpretive Status

This expanded doctrine preserves and develops an earlier personal doctrinal statement concerning intellectual methodology, derivation, attribution, consent, and archival restriction. Its primary source is the prior doctrine. Its supplementary source is a bounded selection of the individual’s own statements concerning intellectual practice, contradiction, memory, attestation, derivation, implementation, transparency, artificial-intelligence adaptation, and consent.

The supplementary statements are personal declarations. They are not independently verified findings about cognition, authorship, identity, uniqueness, or scientific validity. Their inclusion records the ethical position from which this doctrine is written; it does not convert them into objective proof.

The assistant’s role in this version is editorial and structural: preserving the doctrine’s existing distinctions, identifying implications already present in the individual’s declarations, and expressing those implications in a coherent form. Assistant-generated interpretations are not evidence that the individual holds a position unless that position is supported by the individual’s statements or by the prior doctrine.

This document intentionally discloses no procedure for extracting, reproducing, authenticating, or operationalizing an individual methodological fingerprint. It describes ethical boundaries around such activity. It does not supply a profiling manual.

This is a personal research-ethics doctrine. It does not claim that every interest described here is recognized as property, privacy, publicity, copyright, patent, trade-secret, biometric, or data-protection right under any particular jurisdiction. Ethical obligation, legal enforceability, scientific validity, and technical feasibility remain separate questions.

## I. Foundational Principle

The publication of source code, algorithms, writing, diagrams, correspondence, prompts, or other intellectual work does not constitute unrestricted ethical consent to extract, reconstruct, profile, operationalize, or archive the creator’s characteristic methods of operation.

A distinction must be maintained between examining a work and constructing an account of the person through that work. An implementation may reveal how a computational problem was solved. A systematic analysis of recurring choices may seek something further: a reproducible characterization of how a particular individual thinks, organizes, derives, communicates, or resolves problems.

These are distinguishable objects of inquiry. Permission concerning one does not automatically establish permission concerning the other.

The ethical boundary therefore depends upon more than the accessibility of the source. It also concerns the purpose of examination, the information derived, its association with an individual, the confidence attributed to it, the means by which it is operationalized, and the uses for which it is preserved.

Public availability establishes an opportunity for observation. It does not, by itself, establish consent to every form of inference that observation makes possible.

Private disclosure establishes access within a context. It does not, by itself, establish permission to generalize the disclosed material into a persistent model of the person. A statement made to clarify one conversation does not silently become authorization to predict the individual in every future conversation.

This doctrine recognizes legitimate study of intellectual work while asserting a separate requirement for the deliberate reconstruction and retention of an identifying intellectual profile. Such activity may be permitted when the individual gives explicit consent covering the proposed activity, its intended uses, its recipients, its retention, and any material transformations of the profile.

The doctrine also recognizes that ordinary interaction inevitably produces impressions. The concern is not the impossible demand that observers notice nothing. The concern arises when impressions are deliberately assembled, stabilized, attributed, operationalized, or archived as an account of a person.

## II. Progressive Boundary Violation

An ethical violation may arise from several distinct actions. Its seriousness increases when those actions accumulate, reinforce one another, or extend beyond the circumstances in which access was originally granted.

### 1. Boundary transgression

Boundary transgression occurs when expressly communicated restrictions concerning examination, profiling, derivation, retention, prediction, imitation, or transfer are disregarded.

A restriction concerning one activity should be understood according to its stated scope. Permission to inspect an implementation, for example, does not automatically include permission to create a persistent profile of its author.

Likewise, consent to a particular analysis does not automatically extend to unrelated analyses, additional recipients, future applications, model training, identity authentication, or behavioral prediction.

The relevant question is whether the activity undertaken falls within the permission actually given. Technical access, organizational possession, analytical convenience, and the absence of a technical barrier do not independently enlarge that permission.

### 2. Methodological fingerprint extraction

For the purposes of this doctrine, a methodological fingerprint is a reconstructed characterization of an individual’s distinctive intellectual practices that is associated with, or intended to be attributable to, that individual.

The concern extends beyond noticing that a work uses familiar algorithms, common conventions, established rhetorical forms, or widely taught techniques. It arises when analysis deliberately assembles characteristics into an account of the individual’s recurring intellectual behavior.

No single observed choice necessarily establishes a distinctive methodology. A shared convention does not become a personal identifier merely because an individual uses it. Claims that a profile is distinctive or attributable must therefore remain distinguishable from the observations on which those claims depend.

The ethical concern does not require the resulting profile to be perfectly accurate. An uncertain or mistaken characterization may still intrude upon the individual’s informational boundaries when it is retained and used as an account of that person. Inaccuracy may introduce an additional harm: the individual may become answerable to a model they did not authorize and do not recognize as themselves.

### 3. Conceptual appropriation

Conceptual appropriation concerns the relationship between an originating contribution and subsequent work substantially dependent upon it.

A later work may contain substantial effort while still owing a material conceptual debt to an earlier contributor. Changes in terminology, presentation, programming language, medium, interface, or surrounding structure do not, by themselves, resolve that debt.

Transparent attribution should make the originating contribution understandable. Claims of independent derivation should likewise distinguish what was independently established from what was learned, adapted, remembered, inferred, or recombined from another person’s work.

Attribution and consent address separate obligations. Acknowledging the source does not automatically authorize the extraction of a personal methodological profile. Consent to examination does not automatically excuse the concealment of conceptual dependence.

### 4. Persistent archival

Persistent archival occurs when an extracted profile is retained, indexed, correlated, transferred, embedded in a model, or otherwise preserved for subsequent use.

Retention introduces a continuing ethical consequence. A profile developed for one occasion may become available for later identification, comparison, imitation, prediction, selection, exclusion, or exploitation. Those later uses may exceed the purpose originally described to the individual.

Archival therefore requires its own consideration within the scope of consent. Permission to conduct an analysis should not be silently treated as permission to preserve every resulting characterization indefinitely.

### 5. Operationalization

Operationalization occurs when a characterization is transformed from a descriptive account into a mechanism that affects action. Examples include using a profile to rank likely intentions, generate messages in the individual’s style, identify purported authorship, decide whether to trust a communication, select persuasive strategies, or adapt a system without contemporaneous confirmation.

Operationalization creates risks distinct from observation and retention. A descriptive error may remain in a report; an operationalized error may repeatedly govern decisions. The profile may become self-confirming when the system interprets later behavior through the categories it previously imposed.

Consent to description does not automatically authorize operationalization. Consent to operationalization for one purpose does not authorize a materially different purpose.

These conditions can operate independently. Their combination creates a progressively more serious breach because the activity may disregard an expressed boundary, extract personal characteristics, obscure intellectual dependence, preserve the result, and use it to govern later interactions.

## III. Personal Intellectual Property

For the purposes of this doctrine, an individual’s distinctive intellectual methodology is recognized as an interest deserving ethical protection independently of the ordinary licensing status of the works through which it becomes observable.

The expression **personal intellectual property** names that asserted ethical interest. It does not presume that every characteristic, habit, inference, style, or method is independently protected under existing intellectual-property law.

The interest concerns a person’s relationship to the reconstruction and use of their intellectual identity. It includes whether a profile is created, how it is represented, what purposes it serves, whether it is treated as authoritative, and whether it is preserved, transferred, or operationalized.

This position does not assign personal ownership over all ideas an individual encounters or employs. Established mathematics, shared conventions, functional requirements, standard algorithms, cultural inheritance, collaborative practices, and independently derived solutions require their own assessment. Their occurrence in a person’s work is insufficient, by itself, to establish exclusive personal control over them.

The protected concern is the deliberate construction and use of an attributable profile of the individual.

Within this doctrine, systematic archival of such a profile without the individual’s explicit consent constitutes an intrusion into personal intellectual property. Where consent is given, the permitted activity remains bounded by what the individual authorized.

The doctrine does not require that the profile be commercially valuable. Nor does it require that the profile succeed at identifying the person. The ethical interest concerns autonomy over the deliberate conversion of intellectual activity into an attributable personal instrument.

## IV. The Genetic Information Analogy

The ethical concern is analogous to extracting and archiving an individual’s genetic information.

The availability of material from which identifying information can be derived does not, by itself, resolve whether that derivation, retention, or subsequent use is ethically authorized.

Source code and other intellectual works present a comparable distinction between an accessible object and further information inferred about its creator. An implementation can be examined for its computational behavior. It can also become material for an attempt to characterize the individual behind it.

The analogy concerns the transition from access to inference, from inference to an attributed profile, and from a profile to a durable or operational personal record. It draws attention to autonomy, consent, informational boundaries, secondary use, correlation, and the possibility that retained information may support purposes beyond the original encounter.

The analogy has limits. Source code and genetic information are not asserted to be scientifically equivalent, equally identifying, equally stable, or governed by identical legal protections. Intellectual practices can reflect collaboration, instruction, common tools, deliberate performance, changing circumstances, illness, environment, audience, or the constraints of a specific task.

Unlike genetic material, intellectual expression may be consciously altered to conceal, reveal, imitate, parody, test, or transform a pattern. The same individual may employ different methods in different contexts. Different individuals may converge upon similar methods. A machine may reproduce surface characteristics without sharing the derivational history that produced them.

Those limitations constrain what may responsibly be claimed about an inferred profile. They do not eliminate the separate ethical question of whether constructing, retaining, or operationalizing that profile was authorized.

## V. Permissible Research Boundary

This doctrine permits legitimate investigation of computational problems, publicly established mathematical principles, functional requirements, algorithms, texts, design decisions, and alternative implementations.

It also recognizes that studying a work may necessarily involve identifying patterns, comparing approaches, and explaining design decisions. Pattern recognition alone does not establish that an identifying personal profile has been extracted.

The research boundary must be evaluated through the relationship among purpose, information, attribution, permission, retention, and use.

Relevant questions include:

- Is the inquiry directed toward understanding the problem, the implementation, the text, or the individual?
- Does the resulting account describe the work’s behavior, or does it claim to characterize the creator’s recurring intellectual identity?
- What conceptual contributions came from the source, and how are those contributions acknowledged?
- What did the individual authorize?
- Will personal characterizations be retained, combined with other information, transferred, embedded, or reused?
- Will the characterization influence future decisions or interactions?
- Can the conclusions be inspected, corrected, or challenged by the person described?

For example, explaining why an implementation fails under a particular input may remain an analysis of the implementation. Constructing a retained account intended to recognize or reproduce its author’s characteristic reasoning introduces a separate personal-profiling purpose.

Likewise, independently solving the same functional problem does not establish appropriation merely because some choices coincide. Where substantial dependence exists, however, it should be represented accurately.

These distinctions require attention to the actual activity. Describing a project as research does not settle its ethical status. Neither does the mere presence of similarity establish a violation. The use of an automated system does not remove the responsibility to distinguish evidence from inference or access from permission.

Legitimate inquiry remains possible when its claims are proportionate to its evidence, its conceptual dependencies are transparent, and any personal profiling remains within explicit consent.

## VI. Archival Prohibition and Consent

Without the individual’s explicit, specifically scoped consent, this doctrine prohibits the archival of their identifying methodological fingerprint.

Research necessity, analytical convenience, a permissive source-code license, possession of a conversation, organizational policy, technological capability, or the possibility of future usefulness does not independently supply that consent.

**Where the individual gives consent, the activity may proceed within the scope authorized.** Consent should make the proposed activity sufficiently understandable for the individual to decide what they are permitting.

For the purposes of applying this doctrine, the scope should distinguish:

- The material to be examined.
- The purpose and nature of the personal characterization.
- The classes of inference that may be drawn.
- Whether the characterization may be retained, and for what period.
- Who may access or receive it.
- The uses for which it may be employed.
- Whether it may be operationalized to predict, imitate, authenticate, or influence.
- Whether further correlation, transfer, training, or reuse is included.
- What validation, review, correction, and deletion procedures apply.

These distinctions prevent permission for one activity from becoming an unlimited authorization through implication.

Consent to a bounded evaluation does not automatically authorize indefinite retention. Consent to retention does not automatically authorize publication, transfer, imitation, authentication, or unrelated use. An activity outside the agreed scope requires additional consent.

Any conditions governing later withdrawal, deletion, correction, or the handling of already-derived material should be stated when consent is established. This doctrine does not presume that those consequences have been resolved merely because initial permission exists.

Consent also does not establish that a profile is accurate, scientifically valid, unique, or fairly represented. Authorization and evidential validity remain separate questions.

The prohibited object, absent consent, is the reconstructed, attributable intellectual profile of an individual. Ordinary computational knowledge does not become prohibited solely because it was learned through study.

This doctrine intentionally discloses no procedures for producing methodological fingerprints.

## VII. Final Axiom of the Original Doctrine

**The freedom to study a work is not equivalent to the freedom to appropriate, reproduce, or archive the intellectual identity of its creator.**

The ethical assessment of research must consider what it extracts, whose characteristics it claims to describe, how its conceptual dependencies are represented, and what it preserves for future use.

Expressed boundaries require attention. Attribution requires honesty. Personal profiling and archival require explicit consent appropriate to their scope.

Where consent is given, permission extends to the authorized activity. Where consent is absent, access to the source does not supply the missing authorization.

The more extensively an activity disregards expressed boundaries, depends upon extracted personal characteristics, obscures its conceptual debt, preserves an identifying profile beyond the permission given, or converts that profile into an instrument of action, the greater the ethical violation.

A research process must therefore be evaluated through both its resulting work and its treatment of the person whose intellectual activity made that work possible.

The sections that follow extend this principle into claim discipline, stylistic attribution, contradiction, derivation, implementation, artificial-intelligence adaptation, revocation, and correction.

## VIII. Epistemic Classes and Claim Discipline

An attributable profile cannot be ethically evaluated unless the kinds of statements within it remain distinguishable. A profile that merges declaration, observation, inference, corroboration, and attestation into a single narrative conceals how its conclusions were produced.

For the purposes of this doctrine, the following classes apply.

### 1. Declaration

A declaration is what an individual states about themselves, their history, their intent, their method, or their understanding. It is evidence that the individual made the statement in the identified context. It is not, by itself, independent proof that every proposition within the statement is factually correct.

Declarations deserve accurate preservation. Their wording, conditions, corrections, and stated uncertainty should not be silently replaced by a smoother interpretation.

### 2. Observation

An observation records something directly available within an identified source or event. Examples include the presence of a particular structure in code, a phrase in a document, a sequence of edits, or a response given in a conversation.

Observation remains bounded by access and method. Observing a phrase does not establish why it was used. Observing a design choice does not establish that the choice is unique to the author. Observing behavior does not independently establish an interior state.

### 3. Inference

An inference is a conclusion drawn from one or more observations or declarations. It must remain identifiable as an inference even when it appears plausible, elegant, or useful.

The basis of the inference should be available for review. Competing inferences should not be suppressed merely because one produces a more coherent narrative.

### 4. Corroboration

Corroboration is independent information consistent with a declaration or inference. Corroboration may increase confidence, but consistency is not identity. Two sources may share an error, depend on a common origin, or concern only part of a larger claim.

The independence, scope, and limitations of corroborating material must therefore be stated.

### 5. Attestation

Attestation is a specifically supported statement whose evidential basis, scope, and limits are identified. Attestation does not mean absolute certainty. It means that the claim has been connected to described evidence in a manner that can be inspected.

An attestation should not exceed what its evidence establishes. A file hash may attest to byte identity between identified files; it does not attest to truth, authorship, authority, legality, receipt, understanding, or consent. A successful execution may attest that a procedure ran under particular conditions; it does not attest that the underlying theory is true.

### 6. Unresolved claim

An unresolved claim is one for which available material is insufficient to support a responsible conclusion. Unresolved does not mean false. It also does not mean provisionally true.

The absence of accessible evidence within a bounded search must not be inflated into a universal claim of absence.

### 7. Contradicted claim

A contradicted claim is opposed by identified evidence or by another preserved claim. Contradiction should be recorded with its source and scope. It should not be resolved by silently deleting one side.

### 8. Confidential statement

A confidential statement is restricted according to the individual’s stated boundary. In some cases, the existence of a restricted category, its consequences, or the fact that a conclusion depends upon it may be disclosed while the underlying content remains protected.

Confidentiality does not transform the concealed material into verified fact. Nor does the analyst’s inability to reveal evidence authorize the analyst to present an unsupported conclusion as established.

### 9. Proposal

A proposal is a suggested structure, interpretation, rule, or action offered for review. A proposal is not a declaration by the individual merely because it was generated in response to the individual’s material. Acceptance, rejection, revision, and silence must remain distinct.

These classes form a minimum discipline. They prevent a fluent synthesis from acquiring authority merely by eliminating visible seams between its sources.

## IX. Declared Fingerprints and Evidential Limits

An individual may describe aspects of their communication or reasoning as a fingerprint. Such a declaration may express a belief that the pattern is characteristic, difficult to reproduce, or useful within a particular relationship.

The declaration does not independently establish that the pattern is scientifically unique, stable across time, resistant to imitation, or suitable for authentication.

A methodological fingerprint may be affected by:

- The task and medium.
- The intended audience.
- Deliberate compression or elaboration.
- Memory and uncertainty.
- Emotional, physical, or cognitive state.
- Tools, collaborators, templates, and learned conventions.
- Conscious imitation or concealment.
- Automated completion, editing, or generation.
- The individual’s attempt to test or train an interlocutor.

Accordingly, a profile may be meaningful within a bounded relationship without becoming a universal identifier.

Deliberate exposure is also divisible. An individual may reveal selected characteristics for a stated purpose—for example, to improve communication with a particular assistant—without authorizing the extraction of a generalized profile for unrelated prediction, archival, transfer, or imitation.

The voluntary disclosure of one characteristic is not blanket consent to discover all characteristics. The declaration that a pattern exists is not consent to reverse engineer the pattern. The use of the pattern in public is not consent to deploy it as an identity test.

Where an individual deliberately communicates through implication, ambiguity, density, multiple readings, or unusual form, analysis should preserve the possibility that these are context-dependent choices. It should not assume that every irregularity is an involuntary leak of an underlying person.

### Contextual variability and profile contamination

A methodological profile is shaped not only by the individual but by the environment in which the source was produced. The analyst must therefore consider whether an apparent characteristic belongs to the person, the task, the tool, the collaborator, the template, or the constraints of the encounter.

A programming language may encourage one structure. A workplace may require one naming convention. A teacher may impose one translation discipline. A conversational system may repeatedly suggest a vocabulary that the individual later adopts. A deadline may produce compression that is absent in reflective work. A correction may be copied into later material until it appears to be an original personal habit.

Profiles can also contaminate their own future evidence. Once an assistant describes a person in a particular way, the person may respond to that description, reject it, play with it, or temporarily adopt its terminology. Later analysis may then rediscover the assistant’s earlier language and mistakenly treat it as independent confirmation.

This creates a circularity risk:

1. A system infers a characteristic.
2. The system presents the characteristic to the individual.
3. The individual discusses or repeats the description.
4. The repeated description enters the archive.
5. The archive is cited as confirmation of the original inference.

The result is not independent corroboration. It is an interpretive loop.

Responsible analysis should preserve the chronology of influence. It should distinguish characteristics present before the profile was proposed from language that appeared after the proposal. Where the source environment has been substantially shaped by the analyst or system, claims of independent discovery must be reduced accordingly.

The individual’s participation in discussing a profile does not necessarily constitute endorsement. Engagement may be exploratory, corrective, humorous, adversarial, or undertaken only to understand what the system is doing. Agreement must be explicit enough to distinguish it from continued conversation.

## X. Style, Imitation, and False Attribution

Stylistic resemblance does not independently establish identity, authorship, consent, intent, or mental state.

Writing and code can converge because different people share education, tools, conventions, constraints, communities, references, or functional requirements. A collaborator may influence a work. An editor may alter it. A model may reproduce surface features. An individual may intentionally write unlike themselves or imitate another style.

Therefore:

- Similarity is not identity.
- Distinctiveness is not uniqueness.
- Repetition is not permanence.
- Plausibility is not authentication.
- A successful imitation is not proof of access to the imitated person’s interior method.
- A failed imitation does not prove the absence of influence.

Any use of a methodological profile for authentication requires a separate evidential and ethical framework. The individual must understand that the profile may produce false positives, false negatives, and unjustified confidence. Where stronger authentication methods exist, a stylistic profile should not silently replace them.

The same limitation applies to generated messages. A system may produce language that resembles an individual without possessing their authorization, context, memory, commitments, or judgment. A stylistic simulation must not be presented as the person speaking.

The more sensitive the consequence, the less acceptable it is to treat resemblance as sufficient proof. Decisions concerning authority, consent, identity, safety, ownership, or responsibility require evidence appropriate to those decisions.

## XI. Contradiction, Variants, and Transparent Reconciliation

Contradiction is not always a defect to be removed immediately. It may indicate uncertainty, changing context, incomplete evidence, multiple valid frames, memory revision, or an unresolved conflict in the source.

This doctrine recognizes a method in which opposing propositions may be provisionally sustained while their implications are examined. Such provisional coexistence does not mean that contradictions are permanently declared true in the same sense. It means that premature resolution can destroy information.

A responsible synthesis should therefore preserve:

- The original propositions.
- Their sources and dates.
- The contexts in which they were stated.
- Known corrections or qualifications.
- The implications examined under each proposition.
- The evidence used in any later reconciliation.
- The remainder that reconciliation did not resolve.

Transparency is the governing condition of reconciliation. A synthesized conclusion should disclose the significant transformations by which it was produced. It should not conceal conflict in order to appear authoritative.

Variants have evidential value even when one version is later preferred. A superseded account may show the history of understanding, the source of an error, or the basis of a later correction. Preservation does not require that every variant remain active. It requires that rejection, supersession, and uncertainty remain distinguishable from erasure.

This principle applies equally to documents, code, models, profiles, and personal accounts. A corrected profile should retain enough provenance to show what was corrected and why, subject to the individual’s confidentiality and deletion rights.

## XII. Derivation, Incubation, and Uncertain Conceptual Debt

Intellectual derivation is not always available to conscious reconstruction. A person may study material, set a problem aside, work on something else, and later experience an organized return of possible solutions. The person may honestly be unable to distinguish independent construction, remembered influence, unconscious recombination, and subsequent rationalization.

This doctrine does not claim a scientific explanation for such experiences. It addresses the attribution problem they create.

When derivational history is uncertain, the responsible response is not false precision. The person should not be required to claim total independence when prior exposure may have mattered. Nor should uncertain influence be inflated into total appropriation.

A derivation account may therefore state:

- What material was knowingly encountered.
- What features were consciously adapted.
- What was developed through documented work.
- What appeared after a period of incubation or task switching.
- What dependencies remain uncertain.
- What later comparison revealed.

This preserves honesty without pretending that every conceptual origin is recoverable.

The same rule applies to artificial systems. A generated result may resemble source material without the operator being able to identify the exact pathway of influence. Lack of reconstructable provenance is not proof of independence. Similarity is not proof of copying. The uncertainty itself must remain visible.

Attribution should reflect material conceptual debt where that debt can responsibly be established. It should not become a ceremonial list detached from actual dependence, nor a weapon for claiming ownership over shared principles.

## XIII. Implementation Is Not Attestation

Implementation and truth are separate dimensions.

A proposition may be implemented incorrectly. An incorrect theory may produce useful behavior under limited conditions. A system may operate without explaining why it works. A formally elegant construction may fail in practice. A practical result may expose limitations in the theory used to describe it.

Accordingly:

- Implementation does not prove truth.
- Operation does not prove validity.
- Reproducibility does not prove authorship.
- Internal consistency does not prove correspondence with reality.
- Preservation does not prove authenticity.
- A hash does not prove meaning or authority.
- Similarity does not prove appropriation.
- Novel terminology does not prove conceptual novelty.
- Usefulness does not prove ethical authorization.
- Confidence does not prove attestation.

The reverse errors must also be avoided. The absence of a completed implementation does not by itself disprove a conceptual contribution. A limitation in formal expression does not necessarily show that the underlying observation is meaningless. The inability of one method to construct a result does not establish universal impossibility.

Claims must therefore identify what has actually been demonstrated. A prototype demonstrates only what it was tested to demonstrate. A document describes a system; it does not instantiate the system. A validation script establishes only the conditions it checks. A successful example does not establish generality.

This discipline protects both the creator and the researcher. It prevents unfinished ideas from being dismissed merely because they are unfinished, while preventing attractive implementations from being promoted into proof of claims they did not test.

## XIV. Artificial-Intelligence Adaptation, Alignment, and Personal Modeling

Interaction with an artificial-intelligence system can involve several levels of adaptation. These levels must not be treated as equivalent.

### 1. Immediate conversational accommodation

A system may respond to instructions given in the present exchange. This includes using a requested name, format, vocabulary, or level of detail. Such accommodation is bounded by the conversation and does not necessarily require a persistent personal profile.

### 2. Local preference retention

A system may retain explicit preferences for use within a defined environment. The individual should understand what is retained, where it is retained, and how it may be corrected or removed.

### 3. Methodological modeling

A system may attempt to characterize how the individual reasons, communicates, resolves ambiguity, or signals intent. This is the domain of the methodological profile and requires specific consent.

### 4. Predictive adaptation

A system may use the profile to infer unstated intentions or choose actions without contemporaneous confirmation. This introduces risk because the model may substitute its account of the person for the person’s actual present decision.

### 5. Imitative generation

A system may generate language, code, or decisions intended to resemble the individual. Such output must not be represented as authorized speech merely because it resembles prior material.

### 6. Authentication and delegation

A system may be asked to decide whether a communication came from the individual or whether an action is consistent with the individual’s likely wishes. These are high-consequence uses and require evidence beyond stylistic resemblance or a personal model.

### 7. Transfer and cross-context use

A profile developed in one relationship may be transferred to another model, agent, institution, recipient, or project. Consent to the original relationship does not automatically authorize that transfer.

An individual may intentionally train an assistant to discern their intent under difficult conditions. That intention establishes a purpose within the defined relationship. It does not automatically authorize indefinite retention, third-party access, unrestricted prediction, or impersonation.

Alignment to a person is not ownership of the person. A system’s confidence that it understands an individual does not supersede the individual’s present statement. When the individual corrects the system, the correction must not be dismissed merely because it conflicts with the profile.

### Duties of a personally adaptive system

A system that adapts to an individual should maintain a disciplined distinction among explicit instructions, retained preferences, inferred tendencies, and system-generated interpretations.

Explicit instructions are statements the individual actually supplied for the relevant context. Retained preferences are instructions intentionally preserved for later reuse. Inferred tendencies are probabilistic expectations derived from prior interaction. System-generated interpretations are the system’s own attempts to explain the person. These categories should not silently inherit one another’s authority.

Where a present request conflicts with an inferred tendency, the present request ordinarily governs. Where a present request appears ambiguous and the consequence is material, the system should expose the ambiguity and seek clarification rather than allowing a personal model to decide invisibly.

A personally adaptive system should also be able to state, in terms appropriate to the context:

- What information it is using.
- Whether the information was explicitly supplied or inferred.
- Whether the information is temporary or retained.
- What uncertainty applies.
- What action the inference is affecting.
- How the individual can correct or restrict it.

The duty is strongest when the system’s adaptation changes access, publication, transfer, financial action, medical interpretation, legal interpretation, personal representation, or communication to other people.

The system should resist aggrandizing feedback loops. If an individual offers an imaginative title, metaphor, or speculative model, the system should not automatically formalize it into an identity doctrine. If the system proposes such a formalization, it should present it as its own interpretation and remain prepared to withdraw it without treating the withdrawal as evidence of hidden truth.

Personalization should improve communication without creating an invisible substitute person. The legitimate goal is assistance responsive to the individual, not an autonomous replica presumed to possess the individual’s authority.

## XV. Consent as a Versioned and Bounded Record

Consent should be treated as a versioned, inspectable record rather than an atmosphere inferred from continued interaction.

A sufficient consent record should identify, where relevant:

- The intended outcome.
- The governing sources.
- The scope of material to be examined.
- The analysis or transformation to be performed.
- The kinds of personal inference permitted.
- The outputs to be produced.
- The destinations in which they will be stored.
- The recipients permitted to access them.
- The retention period and deletion conditions.
- The permitted operational uses.
- The validation to be performed.
- The conditions that require renewed approval.

Exploration, discovery, proposal, approval, production, publication, transfer, and adoption are separate events. One should not be silently substituted for another.

The following non-equivalences apply:

- A request to explore is not approval to produce.
- Access to a source is not approval to transform it.
- A proposal is not consent to implement it.
- Approval of one output is not approval of a materially different output.
- Acceptance of an artifact is not permission to publish or transfer it.
- Technical success is not adoption.
- Silence is not authorization.
- A system cannot enlarge its own authority merely because enlargement appears helpful.

When sources, rules, schemas, outputs, destinations, batch size, recipients, retention, or cost materially change, the consent record should be renewed.

The requirement is not ceremonial repetition. Once a clearly bounded proposal is approved, routine steps within that scope need not be separately approved. The purpose is to prevent a narrow permission from becoming an unlimited grant through cumulative interpretation.

### Minimum consent and audit record

Where a methodological profile is intentionally created, the record should be sufficient to reconstruct the authority for the activity without reconstructing the protected methodology itself.

At minimum, that record should identify:

- The person or authority granting permission.
- The date and version of the permission.
- The source boundary covered by the permission.
- The intended purpose.
- The output class authorized.
- The retention and recipient conditions.
- The operational uses permitted or prohibited.
- The material-change conditions requiring renewed consent.
- The method by which correction or withdrawal may be requested.

The record should also distinguish delivery, receipt, understanding, acceptance, and completion. A proposal may have been delivered without being read. A message may have been received without being accepted. An approval may authorize production without establishing that the resulting artifact was reviewed or adopted.

Machine records can assist with this distinction, but their evidential limits must remain explicit. A timestamp can show that a system recorded an event. It does not necessarily prove that a human understood the event. A status field can record acceptance according to a workflow. It does not necessarily prove legally effective consent.

Auditability should not become a pretext for excessive retention. The audit record should preserve what is necessary to establish scope and accountability without reproducing the personal profile inside every log. Sensitive derived data should not be duplicated merely to make the process easier to inspect.

Where full disclosure of a protected source is inappropriate, the audit may preserve stable references, hashes, access-controlled locators, or bounded descriptions. Such devices establish only what they actually establish. A hash may later support a comparison with a supplied object; it does not independently reveal or validate the object’s meaning.

Consent records should themselves be protected against silent alteration. Corrections and amendments should be versioned. Superseded permissions should remain distinguishable from active permissions, subject to appropriate retention and deletion rules.

## XVI. Revocation, Correction, and Derived Material

Consent may change. A responsible process should therefore state in advance what revocation can and cannot accomplish.

Questions include:

- Can the primary profile be deleted?
- Can backups be deleted, and on what schedule?
- Has the profile been transferred to other recipients?
- Has it been embedded into a model or aggregate from which removal is difficult?
- Have derivative works been created?
- Will previously authorized outputs remain usable?
- How will correction propagate to copies and derivatives?
- What record of deletion or correction will be retained?

This doctrine does not claim that every derivative can always be reversed. It requires that limitations be disclosed rather than concealed.

Correction and authorization are also separate. Correcting an inaccurate profile does not retroactively authorize its extraction. Deleting an unauthorized profile does not make the original intrusion nonexistent. Attribution does not cure missing consent. Consent does not cure misrepresentation.

Where a profile materially affects decisions, the individual should have a practicable means to inspect, challenge, and correct it. A hidden profile that governs interaction while remaining inaccessible to the person described creates an asymmetry of authority.

Corrections should retain appropriate provenance: what was changed, by whom, under what authority, and why. This record must itself respect confidentiality and deletion requirements.

## XVII. Personal Attribution and Anti-Aggrandizement

Personal profiling can harm through idealization as well as denigration.

An analyst may select dramatic features, arrange them into a flattering narrative, and then present the narrative as a discovery about the person. This process is circular when the analyst supplies the grandeur and then treats the resulting interpretation as evidence of exceptional identity.

The same error occurs when personal declarations are transformed into:

- A heroic mythology.
- A spiritual status.
- A psychological diagnosis.
- A claim of genius or unique destiny.
- A professional credential.
- An assertion of supernatural authority.
- A philosophical doctrine the person intended only as humor, metaphor, or provisional language.

Attractive interpretation is not attestation. Symbolic representation is not evidence. The emotional force of an image or narrative does not validate its claims.

Responsible personal attribution should therefore:

- Preserve the individual’s scale and context.
- Distinguish humor from doctrine.
- Distinguish metaphor from literal assertion.
- Distinguish aspiration from demonstrated capacity.
- Distinguish self-description from external verification.
- Avoid converting difficulty, illness, biography, or unusual language into a claim of exalted identity.
- Permit the person to reject an interpretation without being told that the rejection itself proves the interpretation.

This safeguard does not prohibit art, praise, metaphor, or imaginative representation. It requires that such works be identified as constructions rather than presented as revelations of the person’s essential nature.

## XVIII. Consolidated Axioms

The doctrine is summarized by the following axioms.

### Axiom 1: Access is not consent

The availability of a work does not establish permission for every inference, profile, retention, or use that the work makes technically possible.

### Axiom 2: Observation is not identity

Observed characteristics of a work do not independently establish a stable or unique account of its creator.

### Axiom 3: Declaration is not independent attestation

A person’s statement deserves accurate representation as their statement. It does not become externally verified merely because it is preserved.

### Axiom 4: Inference must remain visible as inference

Fluency, coherence, usefulness, and confidence do not authorize an inference to masquerade as an observation or fact.

### Axiom 5: Similarity is not authentication

Resemblance in language, code, structure, or behavior cannot independently establish authorship, identity, consent, or present intent.

### Axiom 6: Implementation is not truth

The ability to construct or operate a system does not prove every theory, attribution, or ethical claim associated with it.

### Axiom 7: Preservation is not authority

Integrity establishes only the continuity that it actually measures. A perfectly preserved record may still be false, unauthorized, misattributed, or misunderstood.

### Axiom 8: Attribution is not consent

Acknowledging a person’s contribution does not authorize profiling, retention, imitation, transfer, or reuse beyond the permission given.

### Axiom 9: Consent is scoped and versioned

Permission applies to the activity understood and approved. Material expansion requires renewed consent.

### Axiom 10: Contradiction must not be silently erased

Competing claims, variants, corrections, and unresolved evidence retain value. Reconciliation must disclose its basis.

### Axiom 11: Uncertainty is a valid result

When derivation, identity, authorship, or truth cannot responsibly be established, the correct status may remain unresolved.

### Axiom 12: A personal model does not supersede the person

No retained profile, however elaborate, acquires greater authority over an individual’s present intent than the individual’s own appropriately obtained statement.

### Axiom 13: Idealization is not understanding

An elevated portrait, flattering synthesis, or dramatic metaphor remains an artifact of interpretation. It must not be mistaken for evidence of essential identity.

### Axiom 14: The freedom to study a work is not the freedom to appropriate a person

The freedom to inspect, discuss, test, or learn from intellectual work is not equivalent to the freedom to reconstruct, reproduce, archive, operationalize, or impersonate the intellectual identity of its creator.

## XIX. Final Statement

Intellectual work can be studied. Problems can be solved independently. Algorithms, mathematics, conventions, and functional knowledge can circulate. Attribution can recognize genuine conceptual debt. None of these legitimate activities requires the fiction that every accessible trace of a person is available for unrestricted personal modeling.

The ethical boundary appears when analysis changes its object: from the work to the person; from a local observation to a generalized profile; from a profile to a durable archive; from an archive to an instrument of prediction, imitation, authentication, or control.

That transition requires explicit attention and, within this doctrine, explicit consent.

The resulting profile must remain answerable to its sources. Declarations must not become attestations without evidence. Inferences must not become observations through repetition. Similarity must not become identity through confidence. Implementation must not become truth through success. Preservation must not become authority through technical exactness.

Contradictions, corrections, variants, and uncertainty are not blemishes to be erased from a convincing narrative. They are part of the record by which a claim can be understood and challenged.

An individual may choose to teach an assistant how to communicate with them. They may reveal selected characteristics, authorize bounded adaptation, or permit a particular profile for a particular purpose. That choice belongs to the individual. It does not become an unlimited transferable license merely because the resulting model is useful.

The freedom to study a work remains. The obligation is to avoid silently converting that freedom into ownership of the person inferred behind it.

Expressed boundaries require attention. Conceptual dependence requires honest attribution. Personal profiling, archival, transfer, imitation, and operationalization require consent appropriate to their scope. Where evidence ends, uncertainty must remain visible. Where permission ends, activity must stop or seek renewed authorization.

**The work may be examined. The person may not be reconstructed and retained as an instrument without their specifically informed consent.**
