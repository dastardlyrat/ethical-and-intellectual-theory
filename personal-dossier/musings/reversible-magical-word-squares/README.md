# Reversible Magical Word Squares: A Comparative Catalogue

**SATOR / ROTAS, historical double acrostics, cyclic letter squares, and reductive charms**  
**Scope:** Historical and linguistic study; not an instruction manual for magical practice.

This catalogue records nine complete, reversible word squares, one cyclic word-square pattern, and two related magical-letter arrangements. It preserves each letter sequence, distinguishes translations from conjectural etymologies, and explains the formal structures. The purpose is comparative research into **how meaning and symmetry interact**, not to assert that traditional magical claims are true.

> **Translation policy:** A word's meaning, a translator's proposed etymology, and the traditional *purpose of a charm* are three different claims. They are kept separate below. In particular, the speculative glosses of S. L. MacGregor Mathers in *The Book of the Sacred Magic of Abramelin the Mage* are attributed to him; they should not be treated as independently established translations of ancient Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek, or Latin.

## 1. SATOR / ROTAS

```text
R O T A S
O P E R A
T E N E T
A R E P O
S A T O R
```

The opposite orientation (`SATOR / AREPO / TENET / OPERA / ROTAS`) is equally valid. Roman examples are known from Pompeii before the eruption of 79 CE. Its initial purpose and the reading of **AREPO** remain disputed.

| Word | Translation or status |
|---|---|
| **ROTAS** | Wheels (Latin, accusative plural) |
| **OPERA** | With effort, care, or work (a possible Latin reading) |
| **TENET** | Holds; keeps (Latin) |
| **AREPO** | Disputed: possibly a personal name; alternatively a proposed Gaulish word for a **plough** or ploughing implement (Carcopino) |
| **SATOR** | Sower, planter, originator (Latin) |

**Competing readings:**

- **Personal-name reading:** “The sower Arepo holds the wheels with care.” This treats **AREPO** as a name, not as a farm implement.
- **Plough reading:** **AREPO** has been proposed as a Gaulish term for a **plough** (the implement used *for plowing*), which would connect **SATOR** (sower/farmer) and **ROTAS** (wheels) into an agricultural image, potentially a wheeled plough. A rough interpretive paraphrase is “The farmer carefully manages the wheels of the plough.” This is *not* a strict word-for-word translation.

**Status of the plough hypothesis:** Jérôme Carcopino related **AREPO** to the Gaulish agricultural measure *arepennis*, recorded by the Roman writer Columella, and to descriptions of wheeled ploughs in Roman Gaul. A later Greek rendering also glossed **AREPO** as Greek *arotron* (“plough”), but its Latin translation is unreliable. The etymology therefore remains a **documented scholarly proposal**, not a settled meaning of the word.

**Sources for this reading:** [J. Gwyn Griffiths / Duncan Fishwick, discussion of AREPO and Carcopino (1959)](https://cchahistory.ca/journal/CCHA1959/Fishwick.htm); [*On the Origin of the Rotas-Sator Square*, *Harvard Theological Review*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/harvard-theological-review/article/abs/on-the-origin-of-the-rotassator-square1/A81C6B98A6C8352FED7BC5ADDF945AA9).

**Structure:** Five rows match the five columns; 180° rotation preserves the grid. The middle row and middle column both read **TENET**.

**Sources:** [Berlin Papyrus Database — The Return of the Sower](https://berlpap.smb.museum/die-heimkehr-des-saemanns-aegyptisches-amulett/?lang=en); [Corinium Museum — The SATOR Square](https://coriniummuseum.org/2021/07/the-sator-square-by-isobel-wilkes/).

```text

M E T E B
```

| Row | Translation or status |
|---|---|
| **BETEM** | “Internal parts,” according to Mathers's gloss; uncertain |
| **EMERE** | A Latin infinitive meaning “to buy”; not necessarily its meaning *in this square* |
| **TENET** | “Holds” in Latin; palindrome |
| **EREME** | EMERE reversed |

**Historical context:** *Abramelin*, Book III, Chapter XVIII, square 6; presented there as a charm concerning malignant fevers. The association is historical, **not evidence of healing efficacy**. ## 5. SALOM / AREPO

```text
S A L O M
A R E P O
L E M E L
O P E R A
M O L A S
```

| Row | Mathers's *proposed* interpretation |
|---|---|
| **SALOM** | Peace |
| **AREPO** | “He distils” |
| **LEMEL** | “Unto fullness” |
| **OPERA** | “Upon the dry ground” |
| **MOLAS** | Quick movement, or stirring into life |

**Interpretive synopsis:** A peace/life motif supplied by the translator; **not a grammatical English translation of the lines**. The glosses for **AREPO** and **OPERA** here differ from how those sequences are read in the Latin SATOR square. In particular, the **Gaulish plough hypothesis for AREPO** is a separate reading proposed for SATOR, **not** the gloss Mathers gives AREPO in this Abramelin square.

**Historical context:** *Abramelin*, Book III, Chapter XIX, square 9 (affection). Mathers himself notes the strong similarity to the SATOR square. This is the most direct lexical comparison in the present collection.

## 10. EULAMO (cyclic)

```text
E U L A M O
U L A M O E
L A M O E U
A M O E U L
M O E U L A
O E U L A M
```

**Meaning:** **EULAMO** is a *vox magica* (magical name or utterance) with no reliably established literal English translation. Suggested derivations from other languages should not be presented as facts.

**Structure:** This is a **normalized illustration of the cyclic-shift pattern**, not a claim to reproduce a particular tablet's letter placement or orthography. Each row shifts its first letter to the end; columns spell the same sequences as corresponding rows. Unlike SATOR, a half-turn generally changes the text.

**Historical context:** EULAMO-based word squares occur on Roman-period curse tablets, including evidence from Egypt. The form and transpositions are discussed in scholarship on ancient *voces magicae*. See [*Witchcraft and Magic in Europe: Ancient Greece and Rome*](https://eclass-b.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/ARCH310/Readings%20for%2022%20Dec%20and%2012%20Jan/Flint%2C%20Gordon%20Luck%20and%20Ogden%20Witchcraft%20and%20Magic%20in%20Europe%20Ancient%20Greece%20and%20Rome%20Part%20I.pdf).

## 11. ABLANATHANALBA (Greek palindrome)

```text
A B L A N A TH A N A L B A
```

**Meaning:** A historically reported but disputed Semitic interpretation is **“You are our father.”** Its original derivation is unsettled.

**Important technical detail:** In Greek, the conventional spelling **ΑΒΛΑΝΑΘΑΝΑΛΒΑ** is palindromic because **Θ** is a *single letter*. In Latin transliteration, the same phoneme is written **TH**, a two-character sequence. Thus `ABLANATHANALBA` is **not** a character-by-character palindrome in ordinary Latin text, though the Greek-letter sequence **is** a palindrome. The typography and alphabet determine the test.

**Historical context:** Roman-era amulets and magical texts, sometimes in shrinking or wing-shaped arrangements. The [Getty Museum's agate amulet (100–250 CE)](https://www.getty.edu/art/collection/object/103QSR) records a variant and a protective prayer.

## 12. ABRACADABRA (reduction triangle)

```text
A B R A C A D A B R A
A B R A C A D A B R
A B R A C A D A B
A B R A C A D A
A B R A C A D
A B R A C A
A B R A C
A B R A
A B R
A B
A
```

**Meaning:** Its etymology is **unknown**. “I create as I speak” is a popular proposed Aramaic explanation, **not a securely documented historical translation**.

**Structure:** Reductive triangle; one final letter disappears in each successive line. The formal process is *deletion*, not the reversible square symmetry found in SATOR.

**Historical context:** The Roman *Liber Medicinalis*, traditionally attributed to Quintus Serenus Sammonicus, describes this as a fever charm; the claimed therapeutic benefit has no established medical basis. See [CDC, *Emerging Infectious Diseases*](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/25/2/AC-2502_article) and the [American Heritage Dictionary](https://www.ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=abracadabra).

## Structural comparisons

A fully symmetric double-acrostic square, represented by an `n × n` matrix `M`, satisfies:

- **Main-diagonal symmetry:** `M[i][j] == M[j][i]` (row *i* equals column *i*).
- **Half-turn symmetry:** `M[i][j] == M[n-1-i][n-1-j]`.

These are different properties; satisfying the first **does not** automatically guarantee the second. The EULAMO cyclic example demonstrates this distinction.

| Item | Size / form | Rows equal columns? | 180° invariant? | Translation status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SATOR / ROTAS | 5×5 | Yes | Yes | Four Latin words; AREPO disputed |
| SATAN / ADAMA | 5×5 | Yes | Yes | Two interpretable names; no sentence |
| MILON / IRAGO | 5×5 | Yes | Yes | Mathers's speculative etymologies |
| BETEM / TENET | 5×5 | Yes | Yes | TENET is Latin; other readings uncertain |
| SALOM / AREPO | 5×5 | Yes | Yes | Mathers's speculative etymologies |
| ALLUP / URIEL | 5×5 | Yes | Yes | Uriel established; other forms uncertain |
| HOLOP | 5×5 | Yes | Yes | HOLOP gloss speculative |
| ODAC | 4×4 | Yes | Yes | ODAC gloss speculative |
| THIRAMA | 7×7 | Yes | Yes | Mathers's speculative etymologies |
| EULAMO | 6×6 cyclic illustration | Yes | No | No secure literal translation |
| ABLANATHANALBA | Greek-letter palindrome | Not a square | Not applicable | Disputed etymology |
| ABRACADABRA | Reductive triangle | Not a square | Not applicable | Unknown etymology |

### Reproducible symmetry check

```python
squares = {
    "ROTAS": ["ROTAS", "OPERA", "TENET", "AREPO", "SATOR"],
    "SATAN": ["SATAN", "ADAMA", "TABAT", "AMADA", "NATAS"],
    "MILON": ["MILON", "IRAGO", "LAMAL", "OGARI", "NOLIM"],
    "BETEM": ["BETEM", "EMERE", "TENET", "EREME", "METEB"],
    "SALOM": ["SALOM", "AREPO", "LEMEL", "OPERA", "MOLAS"],
    "ALLUP": ["ALLUP", "LEIRU", "LIGIL", "URIEL", "PULLA"],
    "HOLOP": ["HOLOP", "OPOLO", "LOBOL", "OLOPO", "POLOH"],
    "ODAC": ["ODAC", "DARA", "ARAD", "CADO"],
    "THIRAMA": ["THIRAMA", "HIGANAM", "IGOGANA", "RAGIGAR", "ANAGOGI", "MANAGIH", "AMARIHT"],
    "EULAMO": ["EULAMO", "ULAMOE", "LAMOEU", "AMOEUL", "MOEULA", "OEULAM"],
}

for name, rows in squares.items():
    n = len(rows)
    assert all(len(row) == n for row in rows), name
    diagonal = all(rows[i][j] == rows[j][i]
                   for i in range(n) for j in range(n))
    half_turn = all(rows[i][j] == rows[n - i - 1][n - j - 1]
                    for i in range(n) for j in range(n))
    print(f"{name:8} diagonal={diagonal!s:5} half_turn={half_turn}")
```

**Research distinctions:**

1. **Lexical semantics:** Which strings are recognizable words in an independently documented language?
2. **Onomastic meaning:** Which strings are names or divine epithets rather than ordinary vocabulary?
3. **Graphic constraints:** Which strings are supplied mainly to make rows and columns coincide?
4. **Transformations:** Which constructions depend on reflection, half-turn, cyclic shift, or progressive deletion?
5. **Transmission and provenance:** What does the earliest surviving source actually show, and how far removed is a later explanation?

These distinctions prevent formal symmetry from being mistaken for evidence of a unique ancient interpretation.

### Editorial cautions

- Dates apply to **surviving attestations**, not necessarily first composition.
- The *Abramelin* squares are **not** being claimed to date to Pompeii or to originate together with SATOR.
- Manuscript variants exist. The transcriptions in this catalogue follow the cited Mathers edition and should be checked against individual manuscripts before use in a critical edition.
- A geometric match is reproducible; an etymology often is not. Do not promote translator conjecture into linguistic fact.
- Historical charms and medical uses are reported as cultural evidence, not as practical health guidance.
